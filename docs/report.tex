\section*{Background}

Products that must look identical, such as laptop lids, watch cases and phone
frames, obtain their colour from a surface finishing process. Every such
process drifts during normal operation. An acid bath warms up over a shift, a
dye becomes depleted, a gas flow controller reads slightly high. None of these
is a fault. The question is whether the resulting colour still falls inside
tolerance.

\section*{What was done}

A physics based computer model was built covering two finishing routes, and
realistic process variation was pushed through it to predict colour yield. The
model runs the complete causal chain from a change in process setting, through
the film that grows, to the light that film reflects, to the colour a human
observer perceives, and finally to the fraction of parts that pass inspection.

\section*{Principal findings}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em, itemsep=6pt]

\item \textbf{The PVD interference stack is not capable at realistic
tolerances.} It yields 56.4\,\% against a colour specification of $\dE \le 1.0$,
with a capability index of $-0.018$. A negative capability index means the
average part is already a reject, not merely that the spread is too wide.

\item \textbf{A single variable dominates, and it is not the obvious one.}
Nitrogen stoichiometry accounts for 69.8\,\% of all colour variance. Coating
thickness, which is the variable an engineer would instinctively control,
accounts for only 2.5\,\% and never requires tightening. The film becomes
optically opaque beyond roughly $150\nm$, so additional thickness produces no
visible change.

\item \textbf{The corrective action is quantified.} Achieving a capability
index of 1.33 requires nitrogen control to be tightened six fold, from
$\pm 0.040$ to $\pm 0.0067$, and overcoat thickness control to be tightened 2.7
fold, from $\pm 8.0\nm$ to $\pm 3.0\nm$.

\item \textbf{The choice of colour is itself a capability decision.} Holding
the anodizing process, its tolerances and the sample size completely fixed, and
changing only which dye is in the tank, moves yield from 100\,\% for black to
77.7\,\% for blue. This 22 percentage point swing originates in a design
review, not on the production line.

\item \textbf{The two routes fail in opposite places.} Anodized black is
robust in colour but limited by film thickness. The PVD stack has no thickness
problem at all and is limited entirely by composition. A control plan written
for one route is of little use for the other.

\end{enumerate}

\section*{Recommendations}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em, itemsep=4pt]
\item Assess cosmetic capability at the point of colour selection, before a
process is specified, because the colour chosen can dominate every subsequent
process improvement.
\item For the PVD route, direct investment towards mass flow control precision
and in chamber optical monitoring rather than towards deposition timing.
\item For the anodizing route, direct investment towards bath temperature
control and coating thickness metrology.
\item Validate this model experimentally before using it for absolute colour
prediction. Its trends are trustworthy; its absolute colour values are not yet
confirmed against physical samples.
\end{enumerate}

\chapter{Purpose and Scope}

\section{The problem this report addresses}

Consider any anodized aluminium laptop or any coated stainless steel watch. It
has a colour. Now consider that the factory produced a million of them, and
every one must be the same colour, close enough that a customer holding two
side by side cannot tell them apart.

This is harder than it appears, because the colour is not painted on. It is a
by product of a chemical or physical process running inside a tank or a vacuum
chamber, and every such process drifts during normal operation. These drifts
are not faults; they are the ordinary behaviour of a production line. The
engineering question is whether the colour they produce remains inside the
tolerance band.

\begin{keypoint}
\textbf{The central question.} Given how tightly a real production line holds
its process settings, what fraction of parts emerge the correct colour, and if
that fraction is insufficient, which single control should be tightened first?
\end{keypoint}

The commercial importance is specific. A part that fails a dimensional check
fails at the machine that made it, early, when little value has been added. A
part that fails a \emph{cosmetic} check fails at the very end of the line,
after all the value has been added, and in most cases it cannot be reworked.
Cosmetic rejects are therefore disproportionately expensive.

\section{Scope}

\subsection{Included in this study}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=2pt]
\item Type II sulfuric acid anodizing of aluminium, followed by dyeing.
\item Physical vapour deposition of decorative nitride thin film stacks.
\item Prediction of the reflectance spectrum produced by each route.
\item Conversion of that spectrum to perceived colour using the international
standard colourimetric system.
\item Propagation of process variation through to capability indices and
predicted yield.
\item Identification and ranking of the process variables that drive colour
variation.
\item Calculation of the control tolerances required to meet a stated
capability target.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Excluded from this study}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em, itemsep=2pt]
\item Experimental validation. No physical coupons were produced or measured.
\item Mechanical properties such as hardness, adhesion and wear resistance.
\item Corrosion performance and accelerated ageing.
\item Gloss, texture and surface roughness, which contribute to appearance but
are treated here as held constant.
\item Cost modelling and equipment selection.
\item Long term process drift between batches, shifts and operators. Only
within batch variation is modelled. Section~7.3 explains why this matters.
\end{itemize}

\section{How to read this report}

Chapter~2 explains the necessary background from first principles and assumes

familiar with the field may proceed directly to Chapter~3, which describes the

\chapter{Background}

This chapter builds up the four ideas the report depends upon: how anodizing
works, how thin film coating works, how colour is measured numerically, and how
process capability is measured. Each is presented without assuming prior
exposure.

\section{Anodizing}

Anodizing is an electrochemical process. An aluminium part is immersed in an
acid bath, usually dilute sulfuric acid, and connected as the positive
electrode. Passing current causes the aluminium surface to oxidise, growing a
hard, porous oxide skin out of the metal's own surface rather than depositing a
separate layer on top of it.

Two features of this oxide matter for colour:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=3pt]
\item \textbf{It is porous.} The film contains a dense array of fine vertical
pores. These pores can be filled with dye, which is how anodized parts obtain
colour. More pore volume means more dye can be absorbed.
\item \textbf{It is transparent.} Aluminium oxide does not absorb visible
light. Light therefore passes through the film, reflects from the metal
beneath, and passes back out. The colour is produced entirely by whatever dye
sits in the pores.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{defbox}
\textbf{Type II} refers to conventional sulfuric acid anodizing producing films
of roughly $5$ to $25\um$ thickness, which is the standard architectural and
decorative process. Type I uses chromic acid and Type III, often called hard
anodizing, uses colder baths and higher voltages to produce thicker, denser and
more wear resistant films.
\end{defbox}

\section{Physical vapour deposition}

Physical vapour deposition, abbreviated PVD, is a vacuum process. A solid metal
target, commonly titanium, is vaporised by bombardment. The resulting vapour
travels across the chamber and condenses onto the part as an extremely thin
film, typically a few hundred nanometres thick, which is roughly one thousandth
the width of a human hair.

If nitrogen gas is admitted during deposition, the metal vapour reacts with it
to form a nitride. Titanium nitride is a hard ceramic which happens to be gold
coloured, which is why it is widely used on watch cases, taps and cutting
tools.

The colour of a PVD coating arises from two distinct mechanisms, and
distinguishing them is essential to understanding the results in Chapter~5:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.5em, itemsep=4pt]
\item \textbf{Bulk absorption.} The nitride itself absorbs some wavelengths
more than others. Titanium nitride absorbs blue light strongly and reflects red
light well, which is what makes it look gold. This mechanism depends on the
material's \emph{composition}, not on how much of it is present, provided there
is enough to be opaque.
\item \textbf{Optical interference.} Because the layers are comparable in
thickness to the wavelength of visible light, light reflected from the top
surface of a layer can interfere with light reflected from its bottom surface.
The two waves reinforce at some wavelengths and cancel at others. This
mechanism depends strongly on layer \emph{thickness}, and it is the same effect
that produces the colours in a soap bubble or an oil film on water.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Measuring colour numerically}

\subsection{The reflectance spectrum}

The most complete physical description of how a surface interacts with light is
its \textbf{reflectance spectrum}, written $R(\lambda)$. This states, for each
wavelength $\lambda$ of light, what fraction is reflected. A value of $R = 0.8$
at $600\nm$ means the surface reflects 80\,\% of the orange light striking it.

An object appears red because its spectrum is high in the red region of the
spectrum, around $650\nm$, and low elsewhere. Visible light spans roughly
$380\nm$, which appears violet, to $780\nm$, which appears deep red.

\subsection{From spectrum to perceived colour}

A spectrum is not itself a colour. Colour is a perception, and the human eye
does not record the full spectrum. It contains three types of cone cell, each
sensitive to a broad and overlapping band of wavelengths. The eye therefore
compresses the entire spectrum into just three numbers.

The Commission Internationale de l'\'Eclairage, abbreviated CIE, standardised
this in 1931 by defining three \textbf{colour matching functions}, written
$\bar{x}(\lambda)$, $\bar{y}(\lambda)$ and $\bar{z}(\lambda)$. These describe
the sensitivity of a standard human observer at each wavelength. Weighting a
spectrum by these functions and summing produces three numbers called
tristimulus values, from which every further colour calculation follows.

\subsection{CIELAB}

Tristimulus values are not convenient for engineering because equal numerical
differences do not correspond to equal visible differences. The CIELAB system
addresses this by transforming them into three more useful coordinates:

\begin{defbox}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{2.2cm}p{11.6cm}@{}}
$L^*$ & \textbf{Lightness}, from 0 for perfect black to 100 for perfect white. \\[3pt]
$a^*$ & \textbf{Green to red axis}. Negative values are green, positive values
        are red, zero is neutral. \\[3pt]
$b^*$ & \textbf{Blue to yellow axis}. Negative values are blue, positive values
        are yellow, zero is neutral. \\
\end{tabular}
\end{defbox}

A saturated gold finish might be described as $L^* = 76$, $a^* = 4$, $b^* = 49$,
meaning fairly light, very slightly red, and strongly yellow.

\subsection{Colour difference}

To state whether two parts match, a single number is needed. The current
international standard is \textbf{CIEDE2000}, written \dE. Its interpretation
is approximately as follows:

\begin{defbox}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{3.0cm}p{10.8cm}@{}}
$\dE < 0.5$   & Not distinguishable even under careful side by side comparison. \\[2pt]
$\dE \approx 1.0$ & The threshold at which a trained observer can reliably
                    detect a difference. Most consumer product cosmetic
                    specifications are set here or tighter. \\[2pt]
$\dE \approx 2$ to $3$ & Visible to an ordinary observer comparing two parts. \\[2pt]
$\dE > 5$     & Obviously different colours. \\
\end{tabular}
\end{defbox}

This report uses $\dE \le 1.0$ as the colour specification throughout.

\section{Measuring process capability}

\subsection{Why an average is not enough}

Knowing that a process produces the correct colour \emph{on average} says
nothing useful. What matters is the spread. A process centred perfectly in the
middle of its tolerance but with a very wide spread will produce many rejects,
while a process slightly off centre but with a very tight spread may produce
none.

\subsection{The capability index}

The capability index $\Cpk$ combines centring and spread into one number. It
compares the distance from the process mean to the nearest specification limit
against three standard deviations of the process spread.

\begin{defbox}
\begin{tabular}{@{}p{2.6cm}p{11.2cm}@{}}
$\Cpk < 1.0$   & Not capable. The process spread is wider than the tolerance
                 band, and rejects are certain. \\[2pt]
$\Cpk = 1.0$   & Marginal. The tolerance band is exactly as wide as the process
                 spread, giving roughly 2700 defects per million. \\[2pt]
$\Cpk = 1.33$  & The usual consumer electronics gate, giving roughly 32 defects
                 per million. This report adopts it as the target. \\[2pt]
$\Cpk = 2.0$   & Very comfortable, roughly 2 defects per billion. \\[2pt]
$\Cpk < 0$     & The process mean lies outside the specification limit
                 entirely. The average part is a reject. \\
\end{tabular}
\end{defbox}

\subsection{Design of experiments}

Investigating several process settings by varying one at a time is inefficient
and, more seriously, cannot detect \textbf{interactions}, meaning cases where
the effect of one variable depends on the level of another. A design of
experiments, abbreviated DOE, is a structured plan that varies several settings
simultaneously according to a pattern chosen so that individual effects,
curvature and interactions can all be separated from a modest number of runs.

\section{The shared pipeline}

The two finishing routes are modelled independently up to the point at which
each produces a reflectance spectrum. From there they share a single common
path to colour and capability:

\begin{center}
\fbox{\parbox{0.94\textwidth}{\centering
\vspace{2pt}
process variation $\;\rightarrow\;$ physics $\;\rightarrow\;$ $R(\lambda)$
$\;\rightarrow\;$ $L^*a^*b^*$ $\;\rightarrow\;$ \dE\ $\;\rightarrow\;$ \Cpk\
$\;\rightarrow\;$ yield
\vspace{2pt}
}}
\end{center}

Sharing the colour engine is deliberate and is what makes the two routes
directly comparable. Any difference in the final capability figure is
attributable to the physics of the finish itself, and not to any difference in
how colour was computed.

\section{The colour engine}

\subsection{Tristimulus values}

The first step converts a reflectance spectrum into three numbers representing
the response of the human eye.

\begin{equation}
X = k\!\int_{380}^{780} R(\lambda)\,S(\lambda)\,\bar{x}(\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda
\label{eq:tristim}
\end{equation}

with $Y$ and $Z$ defined identically but using $\bar{y}(\lambda)$ and
$\bar{z}(\lambda)$ respectively.

\noindent\textbf{What this equation says.} At every wavelength, multiply three
things together: how much light the illuminant provides, what fraction the
surface reflects, and how sensitive the eye is. Then add up the result across
the whole visible range. Doing this three times, once for each cone
sensitivity, gives three numbers.

\begin{symlist}
\item[$X$] First tristimulus value. Dimensionless. Loosely corresponds to a red
response.
\item[$Y$] Second tristimulus value. Dimensionless, scaled to 100 for a perfect
white. Corresponds to luminance, meaning perceived brightness.
\item[$Z$] Third tristimulus value. Dimensionless. Loosely corresponds to a
blue response.
\item[$\lambda$] Wavelength of light, in nanometres. The integral runs from 380
to $780\nm$, the visible range. The computation uses $5\nm$ steps, which is the
CIE recommended interval.
\item[$R(\lambda)$] Reflectance factor of the surface at wavelength $\lambda$.
Dimensionless, ranging from 0 for a perfect absorber to 1 for a perfect
reflector. This is the output of the physics models in
Sections~3.3 and~3.4.
\item[$S(\lambda)$] Relative spectral power distribution of the illuminant.
Dimensionless. This report uses CIE Standard Illuminant D65, which represents
average daylight at a correlated colour temperature of approximately 6500\,K.
The illuminant matters because an object's colour depends on the light falling
on it.
\item[$\bar{x},\bar{y},\bar{z}$] CIE 1931 2 degree colour matching functions.
Dimensionless. These are tabulated international standards describing the
sensitivity of a standard human observer.
\item[$k$] Normalising constant, defined in Equation~\eqref{eq:knorm}. It
scales the result so that a perfect white reflector returns $Y = 100$.
\end{symlist}

\begin{equation}
k = \frac{100}{\displaystyle\int_{380}^{780} S(\lambda)\,\bar{y}(\lambda)\,\mathrm{d}\lambda}
\label{eq:knorm}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Why this normalisation exists.} Without it, the tristimulus
values would depend on how bright the illuminating lamp happened to be. Dividing
through by the response to the illuminant alone removes that dependence, so the
numbers describe the \emph{surface} rather than the lighting.

\subsection{Conversion to CIELAB}

Tristimulus values are converted to the perceptually organised CIELAB
coordinates by

\begin{align}
L^* &= 116\,f(Y/Y_n) - 16 \label{eq:lab1}\\
a^* &= 500\,\left[f(X/X_n) - f(Y/Y_n)\right] \label{eq:lab2}\\
b^* &= 200\,\left[f(Y/Y_n) - f(Z/Z_n)\right] \label{eq:lab3}
\end{align}

where the compressing function $f$ is

\begin{equation}
f(s) =
\begin{cases}
s^{1/3}, & s > \left(\dfrac{6}{29}\right)^{3}\\[1.1em]
\dfrac{1}{3}\left(\dfrac{29}{6}\right)^{2} s + \dfrac{4}{29}, & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:labf}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{What these equations say.} Each tristimulus value is first
divided by the corresponding value for a reference white, then passed through a
cube root. Lightness comes from the $Y$ channel alone. The two colour axes come
from differences between channels, which is why $a^*$ and $b^*$ are both zero
for any neutral grey.

\begin{symlist}
\item[$L^*$] Lightness, 0 to 100. Dimensionless.
\item[$a^*,b^*$] Chromatic coordinates. Dimensionless, typically in the range
$-128$ to $+127$.
\item[$X_n,Y_n,Z_n$] Tristimulus values of the reference white, meaning a
perfect reflecting diffuser under the same illuminant. For D65 with the 2 degree
observer these are $95.047$, $100.000$ and $108.883$.
\item[$f(s)$] Compressing function of Equation~\eqref{eq:labf}. The cube root
reproduces the eye's non linear response to brightness: doubling the light
reaching the eye does not double the perceived lightness. The linear branch
avoids the infinite slope the cube root would otherwise have near zero, which
would make very dark colours numerically unstable.
\end{symlist}

\subsection{Colour difference}

Two colours are compared using the CIEDE2000 formula:

\begin{equation}
\dE = \sqrt{
\left(\frac{\Delta L'}{k_L S_L}\right)^{\!2}
+\left(\frac{\Delta C'}{k_C S_C}\right)^{\!2}
+\left(\frac{\Delta H'}{k_H S_H}\right)^{\!2}
+ R_T\!\left(\frac{\Delta C'}{k_C S_C}\right)\!\left(\frac{\Delta H'}{k_H S_H}\right)}
\label{eq:de00}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{What this equation says.} At its core it is a distance
formula, the three dimensional equivalent of Pythagoras' theorem, measuring how
far apart two colours are in lightness, colour intensity and hue. The
complication is that the eye is not equally sensitive everywhere in colour
space. It detects a small shift in a pale grey far more readily than the same
numerical shift in a saturated blue. Each term is therefore divided by a
weighting function that accounts for this, and a fourth term corrects an
additional distortion in the blue region.

\begin{symlist}
\item[$\Delta L'$] Difference in lightness between the two colours.
\item[$\Delta C'$] Difference in chroma, meaning colour intensity or how far
the colour sits from neutral grey.
\item[$\Delta H'$] Difference in hue, meaning position around the colour
circle from red through yellow, green and blue.
\item[$S_L,S_C,S_H$] Weighting functions for lightness, chroma and hue. Each
increases where the eye is less sensitive, which reduces the contribution of
that term. For example $S_C = 1 + 0.045\,\bar{C}'$ grows with chroma, encoding
the fact that saturated colours tolerate larger shifts before the difference
becomes visible.
\item[$R_T$] Rotation term. Corrects an interaction between chroma and hue that
occurs specifically in the blue region, where the axes of perceived colour
space are tilted relative to the coordinate axes. It is essentially zero
outside the blues.
\item[$k_L,k_C,k_H$] Parametric factors allowing adjustment for unusual viewing
conditions. All are set to 1 in this report, which is the standard reference
condition.
\end{symlist}

The implementation follows Sharma, Wu and Dalal (2005), which resolves
ambiguities in the original CIE specification concerning how hue angles are
averaged when they straddle the 0 and 360 degree boundary. These edge cases are
the most common source of error in CIEDE2000 implementations, which is why the
verification in Chapter~4 tests them explicitly.

\section{Modelling the anodizing route}

\subsection{Film growth}

Anodic oxide grows electrochemically from the applied current while the acid
simultaneously dissolves it back. The net rate is the difference between these
two competing processes:

\begin{equation}
\frac{\mathrm{d}x}{\mathrm{d}t}
= \underbrace{\frac{M_\mathrm{ox}\, j\, \eta}{z F \rho_\mathrm{ox}}}_{\text{electrochemical growth}}
\;-\;
\underbrace{k_0 \exp\!\left(-\frac{E_a}{R_g T}\right)\left[\mathrm{H_2SO_4}\right]^m}_{\text{chemical back dissolution}}
\label{eq:anodize}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{What this equation says.} The first term is Faraday's law of
electrolysis, which states that the amount of material converted is
proportional to the electric charge passed. The second term is an Arrhenius
rate law describing ordinary chemical attack of the oxide by the acid, which
proceeds whether or not current is flowing. The film grows only as fast as the
first term exceeds the second.

\begin{symlist}
\item[$x$] Oxide film thickness, in metres. Reported throughout in micrometres,
where $1\um = 10^{-6}$\,m. Typical Type II values are 5 to $25\um$.
\item[$t$] Time, in seconds. Reported in minutes, with typical anodizing times
of 20 to 60 minutes.
\item[$M_\mathrm{ox}$] Molar mass of aluminium oxide, $\mathrm{Al_2O_3}$, equal
to $0.10196$\,kg/mol.
\item[$j$] Current density, in amperes per square metre. Reported in
A/dm$^2$, the industry convention, where $1$\,A/dm$^2 = 100$\,A/m$^2$. Typical
Type II operation is 1.0 to 2.0\,A/dm$^2$.
\item[$\eta$] Current efficiency, dimensionless, between 0 and 1. The fraction
of the charge passed that ends up as retained oxide rather than being lost to
oxygen evolution or to immediate re-dissolution at the pore base. Typically
0.6 to 0.8, and modelled here as falling with both temperature and acid
strength.
\item[$z$] Number of electrons transferred per formula unit of oxide formed,
equal to 6. Forming one $\mathrm{Al_2O_3}$ unit requires oxidising two aluminium
atoms, each surrendering three electrons.
\item[$F$] Faraday constant, $96\,485$\,C/mol. The electric charge carried by
one mole of electrons.
\item[$\rho_\mathrm{ox}$] Density of the anodic oxide, taken as
$3100$\,kg/m$^3$. This is deliberately below the $3970$\,kg/m$^3$ of dense
sapphire because the anodic film is porous and partly hydrated.
\item[$k_0$] Pre-exponential factor of the dissolution rate law, in metres per
second per gram per litre. Calibrated so that a bath at $20\degC$ and
$180$\,g/L dissolves approximately $0.05\um$ per minute, the accepted figure for
Type II baths.
\item[$E_a$] Activation energy for chemical dissolution, taken as
$65\,000$\,J/mol. This value governs how strongly the dissolution rate responds
to temperature.
\item[$R_g$] Universal gas constant, $8.314$\,J/(mol\,K). Written $R_g$ here to
avoid confusion with reflectance $R$.
\item[$T$] Absolute bath temperature, in kelvin. Reported in degrees Celsius,
with typical Type II operation at 18 to $22\degC$.
\item[{$[\mathrm{H_2SO_4}]$}] Sulfuric acid concentration, in grams per litre.
Typical operation is 150 to 200\,g/L.
\item[$m$] Reaction order with respect to acid concentration, taken as 1,
meaning the dissolution rate is directly proportional to acid strength.
\end{symlist}

\begin{keypoint}
\textbf{Why bath temperature is the dominant lever.} The dissolution term
contains temperature inside an exponential. Evaluating it shows the rate
roughly doubles for every $10\degC$ rise. A bath that drifts warm therefore
does two damaging things simultaneously: it thins the film, and it widens the
pores. The part emerges lighter and duller. No other single variable produces
this combined effect, which is why temperature control is the first investment
any anodizing line makes.
\end{keypoint}

\subsection{Porosity and dye uptake}

The dye occupies the pores, so the quantity of dye a film can hold is
proportional to the available pore volume, which is porosity multiplied by
thickness. Uptake approaches saturation exponentially with immersion time:

\begin{equation}
c\,x \;\propto\; \underbrace{P\,x}_{\text{pore volume}}
\;\times\; \underbrace{\left(1 - e^{-t_d/\tau}\right)}_{\text{time saturation}}
\;\times\; \underbrace{\exp\!\left[-\tfrac{1}{2}\left(\frac{\mathrm{pH} - 5.5}{1.4}\right)^{2}\right]}_{\text{pH dependence}}
\;\times\; \frac{c_b}{c_{b,0}}
\label{eq:dye}
\end{equation}

\begin{symlist}
\item[$c\,x$] Dye concentration multiplied by path length, the quantity that
actually appears in the Beer-Lambert absorption law of
Equation~\eqref{eq:beer}. Dimensionless as used here.
\item[$P$] Porosity, the volume fraction of the film occupied by pores.
Dimensionless, typically 0.10 to 0.15 for Type II. Modelled as increasing with
both bath temperature and acid concentration.
\item[$t_d$] Dye immersion time, in minutes. Typically 10 to 15.
\item[$\tau$] Characteristic uptake time constant, in minutes. Shorter in a
hotter dye bath because diffusion into the pores is faster.
\item[$\mathrm{pH}$] Acidity of the dye bath. The Gaussian form centred on 5.5
encodes the experimental observation that acid dye adsorption onto the
amphoteric oxide surface peaks near this value and falls away on both sides.
\item[$c_b$] Dye bath concentration, in grams per litre, with $c_{b,0}$ the
nominal value of 3\,g/L used for normalisation.
\end{symlist}

\subsection{Optical response}

A dyed anodic film is a \textbf{bulk absorber}, not an interference filter.
Light crosses the transparent film, reflects from the aluminium beneath, and
crosses back. The Beer-Lambert law therefore applies over a double pass:

\begin{equation}
R(\lambda) = R_\mathrm{surf}
+ \left(1 - R_\mathrm{surf}\right)^2 R_\mathrm{Al}(\lambda)\,
  e^{-2\varepsilon(\lambda)\,c\,x}
\label{eq:beer}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{What this equation says.} Some light reflects straight off the
film's outer surface without ever entering it, and that portion carries no
colour. The remainder enters the film, is attenuated on the way down, reflects
from the metal, and is attenuated again on the way back up. The factor of 2 in
the exponent is that double pass.

\begin{symlist}
\item[$R_\mathrm{surf}$] Fresnel reflectance of the air to oxide interface,
approximately 0.056 for an oxide refractive index near 1.62. This is the
portion of light that never enters the film.
\item[$(1 - R_\mathrm{surf})^2$] The fraction transmitted into the film and
then back out again, hence the square.
\item[$R_\mathrm{Al}(\lambda)$] Reflectance of the aluminium substrate,
approximately 0.92 across the visible range.
\item[$\varepsilon(\lambda)$] Molar absorptivity of the dye at wavelength
$\lambda$. Represented as a sum of Gaussian absorption bands whose positions
determine the perceived colour.
\item[$c\,x$] Dye loading from Equation~\eqref{eq:dye}.
\end{symlist}

\begin{keypoint}
\textbf{A dye absorbs the complement of the colour it appears.} A blue dye
looks blue because it removes orange and red light from the reflected beam. Its
absorption band therefore sits near $600\nm$, not in the blue. This is why the
band positions in the model appear to be in the wrong place at first glance.
\end{keypoint}

Because Equation~\eqref{eq:beer} contains no interference term, a dyed anodic
film barely changes colour with viewing angle. This is the exact opposite of
the PVD stack described next, and the contrast turns out to matter.

\section{Modelling the PVD route}

\subsection{The transfer matrix method}

Because PVD layers are comparable in thickness to the wavelength of light, the
reflection from a stack cannot be computed surface by surface. Multiple
reflections within each layer interfere, and all of them must be summed
consistently. The standard treatment assigns each layer a characteristic
matrix:

\begin{equation}
M_j =
\begin{bmatrix}
\cos\delta_j & \dfrac{i\sin\delta_j}{\eta_j}\\[1.2em]
i\,\eta_j \sin\delta_j & \cos\delta_j
\end{bmatrix}
\label{eq:charmat}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{What this equation says.} The matrix describes how the
electric and magnetic fields of a light wave are transformed on passing through
one layer. Its usefulness is that the effect of a whole stack is obtained by
simply multiplying the individual matrices together, in order.

\begin{symlist}
\item[$M_j$] Characteristic matrix of layer $j$, a $2\times 2$ complex matrix.
\item[$\delta_j$] Phase thickness of layer $j$, defined in
Equation~\eqref{eq:phase}. It measures how much the wave's phase advances in
crossing the layer.
\item[$\eta_j$] Optical admittance of layer $j$, defined in
Equation~\eqref{eq:admit}. It describes how readily the layer supports the
propagating wave.
\item[$i$] The imaginary unit. Complex arithmetic is essential here because it
is what carries the phase information on which interference depends.
\end{symlist}

The phase thickness and admittance are

\begin{align}
\delta_j &= \frac{2\pi\, t_j\, N_j \cos\theta_j}{\lambda} \label{eq:phase}\\[0.5em]
\eta_j &=
\begin{cases}
N_j \cos\theta_j, & \text{s polarised}\\[0.4em]
N_j / \cos\theta_j, & \text{p polarised}
\end{cases}
\label{eq:admit}
\end{align}

\begin{symlist}
\item[$t_j$] Physical thickness of layer $j$, in nanometres. Typically 80 to
$400\nm$.
\item[$N_j$] Complex refractive index of layer $j$, written $N = n - ik$. See
Section~3.4.2.
\item[$\theta_j$] Angle of propagation inside layer $j$, obtained from Snell's
law. It is complex for an absorbing layer, which has no simple geometric
interpretation but is mathematically correct.
\item[$\lambda$] Wavelength in vacuum, in nanometres.
\item[s, p] The two polarisation states of light, referring to whether the
electric field oscillates perpendicular or parallel to the plane of incidence.
They reflect differently at non normal incidence, so both are computed and
averaged.
\end{symlist}

The assembled stack matrix maps the substrate admittance to the front surface:

\begin{equation}
\begin{bmatrix} B\\ C\end{bmatrix}
= \left(\prod_{j=1}^{J} M_j\right)\begin{bmatrix} 1\\ \eta_\mathrm{sub}\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
Y = \frac{C}{B},
\qquad
R = \left|\frac{\eta_0 - Y}{\eta_0 + Y}\right|^2
\label{eq:tmm}
\end{equation}

\begin{symlist}
\item[$B, C$] Normalised electric and magnetic field amplitudes at the front
surface of the stack. Dimensionless and complex.
\item[$\eta_\mathrm{sub}$] Optical admittance of the substrate.
\item[$\eta_0$] Optical admittance of the surrounding medium, air, for which
$n = 1$.
\item[$Y$] Equivalent admittance of the entire coated surface. This single
complex number summarises the whole stack.
\item[$R$] Reflectance, the quantity fed into Equation~\eqref{eq:tristim}.
Taking the squared magnitude converts the amplitude reflection coefficient into
a power fraction, because intensity is proportional to amplitude squared.
\item[$J$] Number of layers in the stack.
\end{symlist}

This is the identical calculation performed by the commercial thin film design
packages Essential Macleod and TFCALC.

\begin{keypoint}
\textbf{A sign convention that silently destroys the result.} Tabulated optical
constants are published as $N = n + ik$, which pairs with one convention for
how a wave is written mathematically. The matrix formulation above uses the
opposite convention and expects $N = n - ik$. Supplying the first to the second
converts every absorbing layer into a \emph{gain} medium, one that amplifies
light rather than absorbing it. The code still runs, the plots still look
plausible, and reflectance quietly exceeds 100\,\%. This error occurred during
development and was caught by an automated check asserting that
$0 \le R \le 1$ for every stack at every angle. That check is retained
permanently, because the failure is otherwise invisible.
\end{keypoint}

\subsection{Optical constants}

Every material is described by a complex refractive index:

\begin{equation}
N(\lambda) = n(\lambda) - i\,k(\lambda)
\label{eq:nk}
\end{equation}

\begin{symlist}
\item[$n$] Real refractive index. Describes how much the material slows light,
and therefore how much it bends and how strongly it reflects. Air is 1.0, water
1.33, anodic alumina approximately 1.62.
\item[$k$] Extinction coefficient. Describes how strongly the material absorbs
light. Transparent materials such as $\mathrm{SiO_2}$ have $k$ effectively
zero. Metals have $k$ of several units, meaning light penetrates only tens of
nanometres before being extinguished.
\end{symlist}

For metals and metallic nitrides, $N$ is obtained from a Drude-Lorentz model of
the dielectric function:

\begin{equation}
\epsilon(\omega) = \epsilon_\infty
- \frac{\omega_p^2}{\omega^2 + i\Gamma\omega}
+ \sum_j \frac{f_j\,\omega_j^2}{\omega_j^2 - \omega^2 - i\gamma_j\omega},
\qquad N = \sqrt{\epsilon}
\label{eq:drude}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{What this equation says.} It splits the material's optical
response into two physical contributions. The second term describes free
electrons, which are what make a metal shiny and reflective. The third term
describes electrons bound in specific energy transitions, each behaving like a
damped oscillator with its own resonant frequency. In titanium nitride it is
precisely one of these bound transitions, absorbing in the blue, that produces
the gold colour.

\begin{symlist}
\item[$\epsilon$] Complex dielectric function. The refractive index is its
square root.
\item[$\omega$] Angular frequency of the light, expressed in electronvolts
through $E\,[\mathrm{eV}] = 1239.84 / \lambda\,[\mathrm{nm}]$. The visible range
spans roughly 1.6 to 3.3\,eV.
\item[$\epsilon_\infty$] High frequency dielectric constant, accounting for all
contributions above the modelled range. Dimensionless, typically 1 to 5.
\item[$\omega_p$] Plasma frequency, in eV. Sets how many free electrons the
material has and therefore where its reflectance edge lies. Aluminium has a
very high value near 15\,eV, which is why it reflects the entire visible
spectrum and appears as a neutral mirror.
\item[$\Gamma$] Drude damping constant, in eV. Represents how frequently free
electrons scatter. Higher values make a metal duller.
\item[$f_j$] Strength of the $j$th bound oscillator. Dimensionless.
\item[$\omega_j$] Resonant frequency of the $j$th oscillator, in eV. This is
where that transition absorbs.
\item[$\gamma_j$] Damping of the $j$th oscillator, in eV. Controls how broad
the absorption band is.
\end{symlist}

\subsection{Stoichiometry as the colour control}

Titanium nitride absorbs strongly enough that beyond roughly $150\nm$ the film
is optically opaque and the substrate beneath becomes irrelevant. Thickness
therefore stops controlling colour. The variable that does control it is the
composition, written $x$ in the formula $\mathrm{TiN}_x$, which is set on the
deposition tool by the nitrogen partial pressure.

\begin{defbox}
\textbf{Stoichiometry.} $x = 1$ denotes exactly one nitrogen atom per titanium
atom, the ideal composition. $x < 1$ denotes a nitrogen starved, metal rich
film. $x > 1$ denotes a nitrogen stuffed film.
\end{defbox}

The model represents the effect of composition through
Equation~\eqref{eq:drude} as follows. As $x$ falls below 1, more free carriers
are present, so $\omega_p$ rises and the reflectance edge moves towards the
blue, shifting the appearance from gold towards metallic silver.
Simultaneously the bound oscillator responsible for the gold colour weakens,
and increased point defect density broadens every spectral feature.

PVD stack across a $\pm 4\,\%$ swing in nitrogen stoichiometry. Right: angular

\end{figure}

\section{Propagating process variation}

Each process input is assigned a control tolerance representing how tightly the
line actually holds it. That tolerance is interpreted as a $\pm 3\sigma$ band,
meaning the line holds the stated range essentially all of the time. Values are
sampled from a normal distribution and each sample is pushed through the
forward physics model. This is a Monte Carlo simulation, using 6000 samples
throughout.

Capability is then computed as

\begin{equation}
\Cpk = \frac{\min\left(\mathrm{USL} - \mu,\; \mu - \mathrm{LSL}\right)}{3\sigma},
\qquad
\Cpk^{\mathrm{upper}} = \frac{\mathrm{USL} - \mu}{3\sigma}
\label{eq:cpk}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{What this equation says.} Measure the distance from the
process mean to the nearer specification limit, then express it as a multiple
of three standard deviations. If that distance equals exactly three standard
deviations the index is 1.0.

\begin{symlist}
\item[$\mu$] Mean of the simulated response.
\item[$\sigma$] Standard deviation of the simulated response.
\item[USL, LSL] Upper and lower specification limits. For film thickness both
apply, at 8 and $12\um$. For \dE\ only an upper limit applies, at 1.0, because
there is no such thing as too little colour error. The second form of
Equation~\eqref{eq:cpk} is used in that case.
\item[$3\sigma$] The conventional denominator. A normal distribution places
99.73\,\% of its values within $\pm 3\sigma$ of the mean.
\end{symlist}

\subsection{Ranking the inputs}

Process inputs are ranked by \textbf{standardised regression coefficients},
obtained by regressing the response on inputs that have first been converted to
standard scores:

\begin{equation}
\beta_i = \frac{\partial\, z_y}{\partial\, z_{x_i}},
\qquad
z_q = \frac{q - \bar{q}}{\sigma_q}
\label{eq:beta}
\end{equation}

\noindent\textbf{Why this ranking and not another.} A raw sensitivity would
report how much the colour changes per degree of temperature. That is
incomplete, because it ignores how much the temperature actually varies in
practice. The standardised coefficient reports how much the colour changes per
\emph{standard deviation} of temperature, and therefore folds together the
physical sensitivity and the current control spread. That combination is
precisely what determines which tolerance is worth money to tighten.

\begin{symlist}
\item[$\beta_i$] Standardised regression coefficient for input $i$.
Dimensionless.
\item[$z_q$] Standard score of quantity $q$, obtained by subtracting its mean
and dividing by its standard deviation.
\item[$\bar{q}, \sigma_q$] Mean and standard deviation of $q$.
\end{symlist}

\section{Experimental design}

A Box-Behnken design is used throughout. For $k$ factors it takes every pair of
factors to all four combinations of their high and low levels while holding all
remaining factors at their centre value, plus replicated centre points.

\begin{keypoint}
\textbf{Why Box-Behnken rather than a central composite design.} A central
composite design requires axial points that lie \emph{outside} the stated
factor range. For a real process bath this is often impossible: the design
would demand acid concentrations or temperatures the line cannot physically
hold, or that would damage the parts. A Box-Behnken design places no run
outside the stated range, at the cost of estimating the extreme corners less
precisely.
\end{keypoint}

The run count is $2k(k-1)$ factor points plus centre replicates, giving 15 runs
for three factors and 27 for four. A full quadratic response surface is then
fitted:

\begin{equation}
y = b_0 + \sum_i b_i x_i + \sum_i b_{ii} x_i^2 + \sum_{i<j} b_{ij} x_i x_j
\label{eq:rsm}
\end{equation}

\begin{symlist}
\item[$y$] Predicted response, either \dE\ or film thickness.
\item[$b_0$] Intercept, the predicted response at the centre of the design.
\item[$b_i$] Linear coefficients. These capture the main effect of each factor.
\item[$b_{ii}$] Quadratic coefficients. These capture curvature, meaning cases
where the response has a minimum or maximum inside the factor range rather than
simply rising or falling across it.
\item[$b_{ij}$] Interaction coefficients. These capture cases where the effect
of factor $i$ depends on the level of factor $j$.
\item[$x_i$] Factor levels in \textbf{coded} units, scaled so that the low
level is $-1$ and the high level is $+1$. Coding matters: on coded levels the
coefficients are directly comparable, so the largest coefficient genuinely
identifies the largest effect. On raw engineering units this would not hold,
because a coefficient in degrees Celsius cannot be compared with one in grams
per litre.
\end{symlist}

\begin{keypoint}
\textbf{The counter-intuitive result.} TiN thickness is the variable an
engineer would instinctively control, and it is almost irrelevant. It accounts
for 2.5\,\% of the colour variance and its tolerance never requires tightening.
The film is optically opaque beyond roughly $150\nm$, so depositing more of it
changes nothing an observer can detect. Composition is the real lever, and
composition is governed by a gas flow controller, not by a deposition timer.
\end{keypoint}

\chapter{Discussion}

\section{Why a control plan does not transfer between routes}

The comparison in Table~5.5 shows that the two routes fail in entirely
different places. The anodizing line requires thickness metrology and
temperature control. The PVD line requires gas flow precision and in chamber
optical monitoring. Neither conclusion is reachable without modelling the
underlying physics, because both routes produce what a process sheet would
describe as a coloured part, and their documentation looks superficially
similar.

This has a practical consequence. An organisation that has developed process
control expertise on one route should not assume that expertise transfers. The
instruments differ, the critical variables differ, and the relationship between
the two differs.

\section{Why thickness is the wrong instinct for PVD}

The result that TiN thickness contributes only 2.5\,\% of colour variance
deserves emphasis, because it contradicts a reasonable engineering intuition.
Thickness is easy to measure, easy to control, and in most coating contexts it
is the primary specification. Here it is nearly irrelevant.

The reason is optical opacity. Equation~\eqref{eq:nk} gives titanium nitride an
extinction coefficient of order 2 across the visible range, which corresponds
to an absorption depth of tens of nanometres. A $300\nm$ film is therefore many
absorption depths thick. Light that enters it does not return. Adding a further
$50\nm$ changes nothing an observer can detect, because that additional
material sits behind a region light never reaches.

\section{Why the colour selection result matters most}

Of the findings in Chapter~5, the dye comparison has the largest practical
consequence, because it identifies a lever that costs nothing to use and is
available earliest.

Every other finding in this report describes an improvement that must be bought:
tighter mass flow controllers, better temperature control, additional
metrology. The colour selection finding describes an improvement that is
obtained by making a different decision at no capital cost, provided it is made
before the finish is committed. A 22 percentage point yield difference is larger
than most process improvement programmes deliver.

The organisational obstacle is that the decision sits with industrial design,
while the consequence lands on manufacturing. Making that connection visible
early is a process engineering responsibility even though the decision is not a
process engineering decision.

\chapter{Limitations}

These results are derived entirely from simulation. Stating the boundaries
precisely is part of the result, and each limitation below is stated with its
consequence for how the findings may be used.

\section{Optical constants are representative, not measured}

The Drude-Lorentz parameters in Equation~\eqref{eq:drude} are representative
literature fit values for each material class. They are not measurements of any
specific supplier's coating.

\textbf{Consequence.} They reproduce the correct colour family and the correct
dispersion shape, which is what a \emph{sensitivity} study requires. The
conclusions of this report concern how strongly colour responds to process
variation, and that behaviour is governed by stack geometry and by the shape of
$n(\lambda)$, not by the third decimal place of any oscillator parameter. The
absolute $L^*a^*b^*$ values reported here should \emph{not} be treated as
predictions of a physical coating. Absolute colour prediction against a real
colour master would require measured optical constants, which the software
accepts as a drop in replacement.

\section{Empirical relations in the anodizing model}

The porosity and dye uptake relations, Equation~\eqref{eq:dye} in particular,
are phenomenological. They were constructed to reproduce published Type II
behaviour rather than derived from first principles.

\textbf{Consequence.} Trends are trustworthy. Absolute dye loadings are not.

\section{Capability computed here is short term}

Samples are drawn at fixed nominal settings, so what
Equation~\eqref{eq:cpk} computes is within batch, short term capability.

\textbf{Consequence.} Long term capability on a real line will be worse,
because it additionally carries drift between bath changes, variation with rack
position, differences between operators and shifts, and gradual bath ageing.
The figures in Chapter~5 should therefore be read as an optimistic bound. A
process that achieves $\Cpk = 1.33$ in this model would require further margin
to achieve it in production.

\section{No experimental validation}

No coupons were anodized, no stacks were deposited, and no spectrophotometer
readings were taken.

\textbf{Consequence.} This is the single largest gap in the study and the
natural next step. A validation exercise of nine anodized coupons, spanning
three bath temperatures and three current densities with colour measured on a
calibrated instrument, would be sufficient to confirm or correct the anodizing
half. The software is deliberately structured so that a measured dataset drops
directly into the existing comparison path.

\chapter{Conclusions and Recommendations}

\section{Conclusions}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em, itemsep=5pt]

\item A single pipeline running from physics to colour to capability can serve
two physically unrelated finishing routes. This makes them directly comparable
on the metric that matters commercially, which is yield against a colour
specification.

\item The PVD interference stack examined here is not capable at realistic
tolerances, yielding 56.4\,\% with $\Cpk = -0.018$. Achieving $\Cpk = 1.33$
requires nitrogen control six times tighter, and requires no improvement
whatsoever in the thickness control an engineer would instinctively tighten
first.

\item Anodized black is robust in colour and limited by film thickness. The
constraint, and therefore the entire control strategy, is the opposite of the
PVD case.

\item Cosmetic capability should be assessed at the point of colour selection.
Changing only the dye, with the process held entirely fixed, moved yield from
100\,\% to 77.7\,\%. That effect is larger than most process improvements could
recover, and it is free to avoid provided it is considered early enough.

\end{enumerate}

\section{Recommendations}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em, itemsep=5pt]

\item \textbf{Introduce a capability screen at colour selection.} Before a
finish is committed, evaluate the predicted capability of the proposed colour
under the intended process window. Reserve pale and chromatic finishes for
applications where the tighter process control is justified.

\item \textbf{For PVD, prioritise mass flow control.} Investment should be
directed towards nitrogen partial pressure precision and towards in chamber
optical monitoring. Investment in deposition timing precision will not improve
colour yield.

\item \textbf{For anodizing, prioritise bath temperature control and thickness
metrology.} These address the two mechanisms that
Equation~\eqref{eq:anodize} identifies as binding.

\item \textbf{Validate experimentally before relying on absolute values.} A
nine coupon anodizing trial with calibrated colour measurement is sufficient to
confirm the anodizing model. Until that is done, use the model for ranking and
for sensitivity, not for absolute colour prediction.

\item \textbf{Extend the model to long term capability} once production data is
available, so that between batch drift can be included and the optimistic bound
of Section~7.3 removed.

\end{enumerate}
